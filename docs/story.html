One sentence to keep in mindWe use genes as clues to work out what each cell is doing, test which clues can be trusted, and do it all without an answer key.

Step 2 · The animal

A fish that heals its own heart

Scientists freeze a small spot of the zebrafish heart with a cold probe. This is called cryoinjury. The muscle cells in that spot die and a wound forms. Over the following weeks the fish rebuilds the muscle.

Drawing, not data. The days match the samples in our dataset: uninjured, and 3, 7 and 30 days after the injury.

Why it mattersWhen a human heart is damaged, for example in a heart attack, the muscle becomes scar and never comes back. Understanding how the fish does it could one day help people.

Step 3 · Inside a cell

Same genes in every cell, but different ones switched on

📚
DNA is a cookbook. Every cell in the fish has the same cookbook, with roughly 20,000 recipes (genes).
📄
RNA is a photocopy of a recipe in use. When a cell uses a gene, it makes RNA copies of it. More copies means the gene is switched on more strongly.
SoLooking at which RNA a cell holds tells us which genes it is using, and so, roughly, what it is busy doing.

Step 4 · The measurement

Reading one cell at a time, as a snapshot

The method is called single-cell RNA sequencing. The heart is taken apart into single cells. Each cell's RNA is read and counted separately, so every cell gets its own row of numbers.

Step 6 · The big measurement problem

We catch only a handful of each cell's RNA

The authors report catching only about 1,600 RNA molecules per muscle cell (docs/data.md). That is a small, random handful from everything in the cell. Common RNAs almost always end up in the handful. Rare ones are often missed, even when the gene is switched on.

Toy simulation, made-up numbers

This imaginary cell has its marker gene switched on, but only 1 in every 2,000 of its RNA molecules comes from that gene. Pull a handful and see whether we notice.

The lessonA zero does not mean "switched off". It can mean "switched on, but missed". With a smaller handful (a badly measured sample), we miss even more. Try sliding to 400.

Step 7 · What "state" means

Type is what a cell is. State is what it is doing.

🧑‍🚒
A firefighter is always a firefighter (the type). But at any moment they can be sleeping, on alert, fighting a fire or training a recruit (the state). All our cells are the same type: heart muscle cells. We want to know their states.

Step 8 · The core problem

The cells come with no name tags

Here are 160 cells from one heart at day 7. We know they are muscle cells and which day they come from. We do not know which state each one is in. That is what we have to work out.

What we really have: grey cells, no labels.

Chicken and eggTo find the clue genes for "dividing", you need to know which cells divide. To know which cells divide, you need the clue genes. Nobody has a trusted list to break the circle. That is what "no ground truth" means.

Step 9 · The clues

Marker genes: clues reported by earlier papers

A marker gene is a gene that earlier studies reported as switched on in a certain state. They are suspects, not proven facts: each paper used other fish, another kind of injury or another method.

Noticenppa and nppb appear for more than one state. One gene alone often can't tell states apart, so we use a group of marker genes per state.

Step 10 · Why clues can mislead

Some marker genes are fakes

The problem slide warns that one published "dedifferentiation" marker mostly follows how much RNA was caught, not biology. Our AI should find such fakes on its own. Here is how a fake can arise.

🌑 Depth
Some samples catch less RNA per cell, like a photo taken in poor light. Among our muscle cells, the day-3 sample Hr11 is the darkest: a median of 659 RNA molecules per cell. Four other samples are close (689–776), and the brightest, Hr3, has 2,317 (results/c001_library_qc.csv).
👯 Doublets
Sometimes two cells are captured as one. Their RNAs mix, so the "cell" looks like it does two jobs.
🌫️ Ambient RNA
Stray RNA from broken cells floats in the liquid and sticks to other cells. A muscle cell can show genes it never used.
🔀 Shared genes
A gene used in several states, or in healthy cells too, can't point to one state.
Toy illustration, made-up cells
How a fake marker can look like an injury response

This toy score goes down when less RNA is caught. All the cells from the dark day-3 sample sit low, so it looks as if day 3 changed the cells. Really only the measurement changed. The test is to compare cells within one sample.

Step 11 · Solving the puzzle

Every cell gets four scores

For each state, we take its group of marker genes and ask: how strongly does this cell use them, compared with other genes? That gives each cell a score from low to high on each of the four states.

Toy cells, made-up scores
Method 1: average, compared with a reference
scanpy score_genes
Method 2: ranking
AUCell or UCell

Why two methodsThey work differently. Method 1 averages the marker genes' amounts and subtracts similar genes. Method 2 ranks all genes inside each cell and asks whether the markers sit near the top. If both agree, we trust the score more. The ranking method is less sensitive to how much RNA was caught, but not completely immune: a dark cell has more zeros, and that still shifts ranks. We will test this on Hr11 instead of assuming it.

Step 13 · Keeping the test fair

The expert's answer key stays locked

If the AI peeked at the expert's labels, the final comparison would mean nothing. We found that the data files you gave us contain those labels. So the first thing the Researcher does is make a clean copy without them. Only the column names were seen, never the labels.

The two questions for the humans 1. With no ground truth, how do we decide which answer to trust?
2. Which checks did the AI run on its own, and which would a biologist add?

Step 14 · What we learned in the first round

First findings: the clues overlap, and one sample behaves oddly

The Researcher read the main papers and checked every suspect marker gene in our data. The supervisor re-checked the key numbers.

1. The data is now safe to use.
A clean copy without the expert's labels exists, made by a tested script. Nobody saw the labels.
2. The papers' clues overlap.
The same genes (nppa, nppb, mustn1b) are named for three different states. From the literature alone, the four states can't be cleanly told apart.
3. The clues are uneven.
Border zone: best supported.
Dividing: clear genes, but caught in very few cells.
Alarm: genes found 6 hours after injury, but our first sample is day 3.
Young again: thin; mostly described by genes going down.
4. A puzzle in sample Hr11.
It caught the least RNA overall, yet nppa takes about 4× more of each cell's RNA there than in an uninjured sample. Real day-3 biology, or stray RNA? The next round tests this.
5. A naming trap. One gene name from a paper (vmhc) was matched to a gene that is almost silent in our data. The likely right match is a different name (myh7l). Gene names change over the years, so every clue needs a name check.

Step 16 · Word list

The words, in plain language

Cardiomyocyte
A heart muscle cell, the cell that beats.
Cryoinjury
Damaging a spot of the heart by freezing it.
dpi
Days post injury: days after the injury.
Gene / RNA
A gene is a recipe in the DNA cookbook. RNA is a photocopy of a recipe the cell is using.
Single-cell RNA sequencing
Counting the RNA in each cell separately.
Sample / library
One heart, or part of one, measured in one run. Our data has 22.
Cell type vs cell state
What a cell is (muscle cell) vs what it is doing right now (alarmed, dividing…).
Injury-responsive
Alarm mode: reacting to the damage.
Border zone
Cells at the edge of the wound, which do the repair.
Dedifferentiated
A muscle cell that partly takes apart its beating machinery and becomes more like a young cell.
Proliferating
Dividing to make new cells.
Marker gene
A gene that earlier papers reported as switched on in a certain state. A clue, not a proof.
Depth (library size)
How much RNA was caught from a cell. Low depth means more genes are missed.
Doublet
Two cells accidentally measured as one.
Ambient RNA
Stray RNA floating in the sample that sticks to other cells.
Score
A number for how strongly a cell uses a state's marker genes.
Ground truth
A trusted answer key. We don't have one.
